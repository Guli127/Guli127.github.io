The parent-visibility switch is the red line

Aug 30, 2026 · 6 min read

In May 2026 I sat down to review the first working build of our school's smart campus platform. I had a long list: attendance, homework, detention logs, leave requests, early-warning alerts, a dozen modules across four role-based portals. But the requirement I pushed hardest for was not on anybody's list, and it took about four words to describe: every record needs a parent-visibility switch, and it is off by default.

It sounded like a small setting. It is the single most important line in the specification, and I would fight for it again.

A record is not a message

When a form tutor writes “didn't hand in the reading task, spoke to him Thursday, watch this next week”, that note has an audience of one: the tutor, next Thursday. It is a working memory aid. The same twelve words, pushed to a parent's phone at nine in the evening with the school's name on it, are something else entirely — they are a communication from an institution about their child, received without context, at a moment nobody chose.

Same data. Different audience. Completely different consequence. A system that treats visibility as a property of the database rather than a property of the moment will flatten every private note into an announcement, and it will do so at scale, silently, thousands of times a term.

If everything is visible, teachers stop writing things down

This is the argument that usually ends the discussion, because it lands on something engineers care about: data quality.

Teachers are not naive about who reads what. The week after a system starts broadcasting notes to parents, the notes change. “Struggling with confidence in class discussion” becomes “participation: satisfactory”. The detail that made the record useful — the thing you would actually want a colleague to know before they teach that child on Monday — quietly disappears, because writing it down now has a cost the teacher did not agree to pay.

And every downstream feature is built on those records. The early-warning alerts, the mastery trends, the monthly reports, the AI layer everyone is excited about: all of it reads from a table that teachers have now learned to fill with nothing. A transparency feature that empties the database is not a transparency feature. It is a very expensive way to make a school less informed about its own students.

Visibility should be a decision with a name attached

Default-on means nobody decided. The record became visible because a migration ran. Nobody is accountable for the timing, the framing, or the conversation that follows — and there is always a conversation that follows.

Default-off plus an explicit action means there is a person who decided that this parent should see this record now, and who is ready for the phone call. In practice that is the same moment the tutor would have picked up the phone anyway. The switch does not hide information from parents; it puts the disclosure back inside a relationship where somebody is responsible for it.

The objection I take seriously

Opacity protects institutions, not children, and parents have a real right to know how their child is doing. I agree with that, and it is exactly why the switch is a decision point rather than a lock. Parents get the full picture through the channels built for it — reports, meetings, the tutor who answers the phone. What they should not get is an unfiltered feed of internal working notes, which is not transparency but an abdication dressed as it.

Why this generalises

I am now building an AI agent for the marking loop, and the same principle shows up as design principle 03: an AI-generated grade or comment never reaches a student until a teacher has accepted, edited or overridden it. Different feature, identical structure. The system may do the work; a person owns the moment it becomes real to a child.

The general lesson I took from that review is this. In school software, the interesting question is not “who can see this record”. It is who is accountable for the moment it becomes visible. And because most people never change a default, every default in an education product is a policy decision — usually made by whoever wrote the schema, on a Tuesday, without knowing they were making it.

That is a strange amount of power to leave lying in a config file. Someone in the room should have taught a class.

家长可见性开关，是那条红线

2026 年 8 月 30 日 · 阅读约 6 分钟

2026 年 5 月，我坐下来评审学校智慧校园平台的第一个可运行版本。我准备了一份很长的清单：考勤、作业、留堂记录、外出请假、异常预警——四个角色端、十几个模块。但我最坚持的那一条，不在任何人的清单上，而且一句话就能说完：每一条记录都要有一个「家长可见」开关，并且默认关闭。

它听起来只是一个小设置。但它是整份需求文档里最重要的一行，换一次我还会这么争。

一条记录，不等于一条消息

当班主任写下「阅读任务没交，周四找他谈过了，下周盯一下」，这条备注的受众只有一个人：下周四的他自己。它是一份工作备忘。而同样这十几个字，晚上九点带着学校的名字推送到家长手机上，性质就完全不同了——那是一所机构关于他们孩子发出的正式沟通，在没有上下文、没有人挑选过的时刻抵达。

同样的数据，不同的受众，完全不同的后果。如果一个系统把「可见性」当成数据库的属性、而不是某个具体时刻的属性，它就会把每一条私下的记录压平成一次公开通告——而且是无声地、成规模地、一学期上千次地做这件事。

如果什么都可见，老师就不再写了

这是通常能结束争论的那条理由，因为它落在工程师真正在意的东西上：数据质量。

老师对「谁会看到什么」从来不天真。系统开始把备注同步给家长的第二周，备注本身就会变。「课堂讨论中表现不自信」会变成「课堂参与：良好」。那些真正让记录有用的细节——你希望周一进班的同事事先知道的那句话——就悄悄消失了，因为写下它现在有了一份老师并没有同意支付的成本。

而所有下游功能都建在这些记录之上：异常预警、掌握度趋势、月度报告，以及所有人都很兴奋的那层 AI 能力。它们读取的，是一张老师已经学会「什么都不填」的表。一个把数据库掏空的透明化功能，不叫透明化，它只是一种代价高昂的、让学校对自己学生更不了解的方式。

可见，应该是一个有名字的决定

默认开启，意味着没有人做过决定。记录变得可见，只是因为跑了一次数据迁移。没有人对时机、措辞，以及随之而来的那场对话负责——而那场对话总是会发生。

默认关闭 + 一次明确的操作，意味着有一个人决定了：这位家长，应该在此刻看到这条记录，并且他做好了接那个电话的准备。而在实际工作中，那恰好就是班主任本来也会拿起电话的时刻。这个开关并没有对家长隐藏信息，它只是把「告知」这件事放回一段有人负责的关系里。

我认真对待的那个反驳

不透明保护的是机构，不是孩子；家长确实有权知道孩子的状况。我同意——这也正是为什么这个开关是一个决策点，而不是一把锁。家长应当通过为此设计的渠道获得完整信息：报告、面谈、以及那个会接电话的班主任。他们不该获得的，是一条未经过滤的内部工作记录流——那不是透明，那是把责任卸下来，再给它披一件透明的外衣。

为什么这条规则可以推广

我现在在做一个面向批改闭环的 AI Agent，同一条原则在那里表现为设计原则 03：AI 生成的分数或评语，在教师采纳、修改或推翻之前，绝不会到达学生。功能不同，结构完全一样。系统可以做事，但「它对一个孩子成为现实」的那一刻，必须由人来承担。

我从那次评审里带走的普遍结论是：在校园软件里，值得问的不是「谁能看到这条记录」，而是「它变为可见的那一刻，谁来负责」。并且，因为绝大多数人永远不会去改默认值，教育产品里的每一个默认值都是一次政策决定——通常是由某个写 schema 的人，在某个周二，在完全不知道自己正在做政策决定的情况下做出的。

把这么大的权力留在一个配置文件里，是件挺奇怪的事。那个房间里，应该有一个上过课的人。
