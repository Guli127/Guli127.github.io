Both sides were reasonable. The requirement was broken.

Aug 30, 2026 · 5 min read

I've watched school software fail in two directions, and they are almost mirror images of each other.

Failure one: the wishlist nobody can build

Teachers are asked what they want, and they answer honestly. The result is a document full of sentences like “the system should automatically remind the relevant teacher when a student's performance is abnormal”. Every word of that is reasonable, and none of it is buildable. What counts as abnormal? Compared to whom? Over what window? Who is the relevant teacher when a student is failing one subject and thriving in four? Does the reminder go out at midnight?

An engineer receiving that sentence has to invent answers to five questions they are not qualified to answer, and they will — quietly, in code, in an afternoon. The teacher then finds the feature useless and concludes that the system doesn't understand schools. Both people behaved reasonably. The requirement was the thing that was broken.

Failure two: the elegant feature nobody opens

Now the mirror image. A good engineering team, working from clean principles, builds a beautifully general gradebook with configurable everything. It takes eleven clicks to record that a student didn't hand in their homework. A teacher has thirty students and four minutes between lessons. By week three they are recording it in a notebook again, and the expensive system is a very pretty read-only view of nothing.

Nobody on that team did anything wrong either. They just never had to use the thing they made on a Thursday, with a queue at the door.

Why more meetings don't fix it

The standard remedy is to put both groups in a room more often. It helps a little, and it has a ceiling, because the two groups don't fail at communicating — they fail at knowing which details matter. The teacher can't tell which half of their sentence is the hard half. The engineer can't tell which of their fifteen reasonable defaults is the one that will empty the database. Neither can flag what they don't know they know.

What closes that gap reliably is not a better meeting. It is one person who has done both jobs, holding both models at once.

Three examples from my own specs

The OCR output has to stay editable. When I specified photo capture for my marking agent, the natural design was: photo in, text out, grade out. I insisted the extracted text remain editable before anything downstream runs. Not because editing is a nice feature — because a misread “not” in a student's answer becomes a lost mark, becomes a wrong error diagnosis, becomes the wrong practice question, and the teacher never sees where it went wrong. One affordance stops an error from travelling.

The total is added up outside the model. Language models are unreliable at arithmetic in a way that is easy to forget when the rest of the output looks fluent. A teacher who has ever returned a paper with the wrong total knows exactly how much that costs in trust — one visible slip and the next twenty correct grades get re-checked by hand, which is worse than no tool at all.

The parent-visibility switch defaults to off. Not a privacy checkbox for its own sake, but because a teacher who thinks a parent is reading writes worse notes, and every warning feature we wanted to build reads from those notes. I've written about that one separately.

None of those three came out of a model choice or an architecture decision. All three came from having stood in the classroom the feature lands in. And all three are the kind of thing that sounds obvious the moment someone says it out loud — which is precisely why you need someone in the room who will.

The uncomfortable half

The obligation runs both ways, and this is the part teachers like less. If I want a say in how the system is built, I have to be able to build. Not at a professional engineer's level — but well enough to know what I'm asking for, well enough that “can we just…” is a question I've already half-answered, and well enough to prototype the thing instead of describing it for six months.

That is what I'm doing now, and it is why there is a working prototype on the front page of this site rather than a slide about one.

两边都没做错，坏掉的是那条需求

2026 年 8 月 30 日 · 阅读约 5 分钟

我见过校园软件从两个相反的方向失败，而它们几乎是彼此的镜像。

失败一：没人能实现的愿望清单

有人来问老师想要什么，老师如实回答。于是文档里出现这样的句子：「当学生表现异常时，系统应自动提醒相关老师。」这句话每个字都合理，但整句都无法实现。什么叫异常？和谁比？看多长的窗口？一个学生一门挂科、四门优秀时，「相关老师」是谁？提醒是不是半夜也发？

拿到这句话的工程师，必须替五个他没有资格回答的问题发明答案——而他会的，安静地，在代码里，一个下午就完成了。老师随后发现这个功能没用，并得出结论：这系统不懂学校。两个人的行为都合理，坏掉的是那条需求本身。

失败二：没人点开的优雅功能

再看镜像的另一面。一支优秀的工程团队，从干净的原则出发，做出一个高度通用、什么都可配置的成绩册。记录「某个学生作业没交」需要点十一下。而老师带三十个学生，两节课之间只有四分钟。到第三周，他又开始记在本子上了，那套昂贵的系统变成一个非常好看的、只读的空视图。

这个团队也没有做错什么。他们只是从来不必在某个周四、门口还排着队的时候，使用自己做出来的东西。

为什么多开会解决不了

常见的解法是让两拨人多进一个会议室。这有用，但很快触顶——因为这两拨人失败的地方不是「沟通不畅」，而是「不知道哪个细节要紧」。老师说不出自己那句话里，难的是哪半句；工程师说不出自己那十五个合理的默认值里，哪一个会把数据库掏空。他们都无法标出自己「不知道自己知道」的东西。

能稳定补上这条缝的，不是一场更好的会议，而是一个两边都干过、同时装着两套模型的人。

我自己写的三条需求

OCR 的输出必须保持可编辑。给批改 Agent 写拍照上传这一步时，最自然的设计是：照片进，文本出，分数出。我坚持要求识别出的文本在任何下游步骤运行之前保持可编辑。不是因为「可编辑」是个好功能，而是因为学生答案里被识错的一个 not，会变成一分丢失，变成一个错误的错因判断，变成一道错的练习题——而老师永远看不到它是从哪一步歪掉的。一个小小的可编辑，拦住了一个会长途旅行的错误。

总分在模型之外计算。语言模型在算术上的不可靠，很容易被它其余部分流畅的输出掩盖过去。任何一个曾经把总分算错、还把卷子发下去过的老师，都清楚这在信任上的代价：一次可见的失误之后，接下来二十个正确的分数都会被人工重新核对一遍——那比没有这个工具还糟。

家长可见性开关默认关闭。这不是为了隐私而勾的一个复选框，而是因为：一个认为家长正在阅读的老师，会把记录写得更差；而我们想做的每一个预警功能，读的都是那些记录。这一条我单独写过。

这三条，没有一条来自模型选型或架构决策。三条全都来自「我站在这个功能最终落地的那间教室里」。而且三条在被说出口的那一刻都显得理所当然——这恰恰是为什么，房间里需要有一个会把它说出口的人。

不太舒服的那一半

这份义务是双向的，而这半边是老师们不太喜欢的部分。如果我想在系统怎么做上有发言权，我就必须能动手做。不需要达到职业工程师的水平，但要好到足以知道自己在要什么，好到「能不能顺手加个……」这种问题我自己已经答了一半，也好到可以把东西直接做出来，而不是用六个月去描述它。

这就是我现在在做的事，也是为什么这个网站首页上放的是一个能点的原型，而不是一页讲原型的 slide。
